RM0390 §6.3.2 · RCC_PLLCFGR · HSI 16 MHz → 180 MHz

A fast clock that wanders, kept honest by a slow one.

You cannot get a steady 180 MHz straight from a small, cheap oscillator. A PLL builds one from two parts: a fast oscillator that drifts, and a slow reference that does not. Every few ticks it checks the fast one against the slow one and nudges it back.

01 · the problem

Steady or fast. Not both.

Each kind of clock is good at one thing. The PLL takes the steady part from one and the fast part from the other.

General background, not from RM0390. The one fact from this repo: the 16 MHz HSI is an internal RC oscillator, not a crystal, so "steady" here means steady enough to anchor the PLL. The manual does not describe the PLL's inner loop.

02 · lock lab

What the slow beat buys you

Grey line: the fast oscillator on its own. Orange line: the same oscillator with the PLL checking it. Each thin vertical tick is one check against the reference. Press Heat the chip and watch what happens to each line.

Illustrative model, not a measurement. The "oscillator" wanders randomly and, if you heat it, slides at a steady rate. At every reference tick the loop measures the average error of the last period and subtracts most of it. The real STM32 loop filter is different and is not described in RM0390. What carries over: between two checks the oscillator is on its own, so a longer gap lets it wander farther. Error is shown in ppm (parts per million): 100 ppm of 180 MHz is 18 kHz.

04 · where else

Where PLLs show up

Any time a device needs a fast clock it can trust, or needs to pick one of many clocks by changing a number.

This chip: system clockThe main PLL makes SYSCLK. P is the last divider before it (SVD: RCC_PLLCFGR.PLLM, PLLN, PLLP).
This chip: USB, SDIO, random numbersThe same VCO feeds a second divider, PLLQ, for these peripherals (SVD description of PLLQ).
This chip: audioA second PLL called PLLI2S exists for audio clocks (SVD name for the PLLSRC field).
Radios (general)Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and FM radios choose a channel by changing N. The dial is one number.
Computers and phones (general)CPU and memory clocks are built from a slow reference the same way, and can be changed while running.
Data links (general)A receiver can use a PLL to recover the sender's clock from the data itself.
05 · easy to get wrong

Gotchas (RM0390 p. 130)

M, N and P only change while the PLL is offThe manual says these bits "can be written only if PLL is disabled". Write them first, then turn the PLL on.
Reference must be 1 to 2 MHzOutside that range the setting is a "wrong configuration". 2 MHz is "recommended … to limit PLL jitter".
VCO must be 100 to 432 MHzThe window caps how high you can go. Also, P can be 2, 4, 6 or 8, never 1. So the VCO must run at least twice as fast as SYSCLK, and that is why the chain goes up to 360 and back down to 180.
N has its own range50 to 432. Below 100 is only allowed when the reference is above 1 MHz, and the VCO must still reach 100 MHz.
P must not push SYSCLK past 180 MHzThe manual leaves this check to you.
Locking takes timeAfter you turn the PLL on, it needs time to lock. The next step waits for the PLLRDY flag before using it.
