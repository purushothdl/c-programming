SPI: a shared clock instead of a shared baud rate

Where UART needs both sides to guess the same timing, SPI just sends the timing along. One wire carries the clock, and every clock edge says exactly when to put a bit on the wire and when to read it.

The four wires

SCLK serial clockDriven by the master only. Every pulse moves one bit each way. No clock, no data, so the slave never needs its own baud generator.
MOSI master out, slave inMaster's data to the slave. Driven by master, read by slave.
MISO master in, slave outSlave's data to the master. Driven by slave, read by master, at the same time as MOSI (full duplex).
CS / SS chip select, active lowMaster pulls it low to wake one slave and frames the transfer. High = slave ignores the bus and releases MISO (Hi-Z). One CS per slave.

Inside each chip sits an 8-bit shift register. The two registers are wired in a ring (master out → slave in, slave out → master in). After 8 clock pulses the two bytes have swapped places. Every SPI read is also a write, and vice versa.

    Why sample and shift on opposite edges?

    Both chips change their output and read their input off the same clock, but wires and gates add small, unpredictable delays. Splitting the two jobs gives the data a half-clock safety margin.

    The speed is identical: one bit per clock cycle either way. Opposite edges only buy margin. Receivers need the input stable for a short setup time before the sampling edge and a hold time after it, and a half-clock gap makes both easy to meet.

    Why there's no clock drift here

    UART's problem was two independent clocks slowly disagreeing. In SPI the slave doesn't count time at all. It just reacts to edges the master produces, so master and slave can never fall out of step, and you can run it from kHz to tens of MHz. What can go wrong is agreeing on which edge to use. Untick "same mode" above, set the slave to a different CPHA, and watch the bits get read at the wrong moment (or while the line is still floating).
